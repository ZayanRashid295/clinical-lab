# Medicine (Internal) — Dermatology (Infestations) – Scabies Diagnostic Test
## Topic: Dermatology – Infestations → Scabies

## Question
A 28-year-old patient presents with intense nocturnal pruritus and linear burrows between the fingers. On examination, you observe multiple serpiginous tracks on the web spaces of the hands. To confirm your clinical suspicion of scabies, which diagnostic test would most reliably provide definitive evidence?

## Options and Explanations

**A. Skin biopsy**

### Choice A Explanation
Skin biopsy can detect mites in tissue, but it is invasive, not routinely required, and often reserved for atypical or complicated cases.  
It is not the first-line confirmatory test.

**B. Dermoscopy alone**

### Choice B Explanation
Dermoscopy can visualize the “delta-wing jet” sign (mite head), providing a rapid, non-invasive clue, but it is operator-dependent and less definitive than direct microscopy.

**C. Complete blood count**

### Choice C Explanation
CBC may show secondary eosinophilia, but it is non-specific and cannot confirm scabies infestation.

**D. Skin scraping and microscopy ✅**

### Choice D Explanation
Skin scraping followed by light microscopy is the Gold Standard diagnostic test for scabies.  
Procedure: Apply mineral oil on burrows, scrape the lesion, examine under microscope for mites, eggs, or scybala (mite fecal pellets).  
This method provides direct visualization of the causative agent, confirming the diagnosis definitively.  
Advantages: Rapid, cost-effective, highly specific, and widely available.

**E. Patch test**

### Choice E Explanation
Patch testing is used to detect allergic contact dermatitis and is irrelevant in diagnosing parasitic infestations like scabies.

**Correct Answer:** D

## Explanation

### Keywords in the Stem to Identify the Correct Option
- **“Linear burrows / serpiginous tracks”** – Classic pathognomonic lesion of scabies; points toward a definitive diagnostic test that identifies the mite directly.  
- **“Confirm your clinical suspicion”** – Indicates the need for a definitive, confirmatory test rather than supportive or suggestive tests.  

Classic Triad in Stem (for scabies):  
- Intense nocturnal pruritus  
- Burrows between fingers/web spaces  
- Distribution pattern on hands/skin folds  

These elements together point directly toward skin scraping and microscopy as the Gold Standard confirmatory test.

## Choice-by-Choice Explanations

Scabies is an ectoparasitic infestation caused by *Sarcoptes scabiei* var. *hominis*. Definitive diagnosis is made by identifying mites, eggs, or fecal pellets via skin scraping and microscopy, while clinical features (pruritus, burrows) guide suspicion.  

### Dermatology – Infestations → Scabies

| Feature | Clinical Presentation / Details | Key Points / Notes |
| ------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------- |
| Etiology | • *Sarcoptes scabiei* var. *hominis* <br>• Obligate human ectoparasite <br>• Burrows into stratum corneum <br>• Transmitted via close contact |  |
| Symptoms | • Intense nocturnal pruritus <br>• Often worse at night <br>• May involve family contacts <br>• Pruritus caused by hypersensitivity to mite and feces <br>• Can trigger excoriations and secondary infection |  |
| Signs | • Linear burrows <br>• Serpiginous tracks <br>• Papules and vesicles <br>• Commonly on web spaces, wrists, elbows, waist, genitalia <br>• Classic sites: interdigital spaces, flexural surfaces <br>• Nodular scabies may occur in genital region |  |
| Diagnosis | • Clinical suspicion from history and signs <br>• Skin scraping and microscopy (Gold Standard) <br>• Dermoscopy as supportive <br>• Biopsy rarely required <br>• CBC nonspecific |  |
| Treatment / Management | • Topical permethrin 5% cream <br>• Oral ivermectin (severe/crusted cases) <br>• Treat close contacts <br>• Wash bedding and clothes <br>• Apply permethrin from neck down, repeat in 7 days <br>• Ivermectin 200 µg/kg orally, repeat in 1–2 weeks <br>• Avoid corticosteroids unless severe inflammation |  |
| Complications | • Secondary bacterial infection (impetigo, cellulitis) <br>• Post-scabetic nodules <br>• Crusted scabies in immunocompromised <br>• Early treatment prevents secondary infection <br>• Crusted scabies requires prolonged therapy and isolation |  |

### Differential Diagnosis (DDX)

| Condition | Clinical Features | Distinguishing Points / Notes |
| --------- | ---------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Eczema / Atopic Dermatitis | • Intense pruritus <br>• Erythematous patches <br>• Dry, lichenified skin <br>• Flexural distribution <br>• Usually chronic and recurrent | • No burrows <br>• Often associated with personal/family atopy |
| Contact Dermatitis | • Pruritus <br>• Localized erythema <br>• Vesicles or papules <br>• Often linear pattern from contact <br>• History of exposure to irritant/allergen <br>• Lesions confined to contact area | • Resolves with avoidance or corticosteroids <br>• No burrows, not serpiginous |
| Pediculosis (Head or Body Lice) | • Intense pruritus, often worse at night <br>• Nits or lice visible on hair shafts or clothing seams <br>• Excoriations | • Involves hair or clothing rather than finger webs <br>• No burrows in skin |
| Papular Urticaria (Insect Bite Reaction) | • Intensely itchy papules <br>• Often grouped or scattered <br>• May show central punctum <br>• Common on exposed areas <br>• History of insect bites | • Lesions transient (hours–days) <br>• No burrows, linear tracks absent |
| Folliculitis | • Pruritic pustules or papules <br>• Hair follicle involvement <br>• May have surrounding erythema | • Often bacterial in origin <br>• No serpiginous burrows <br>• Usually localized to hair-bearing areas |
| Norwegian / Crusted Scabies | • Thick crusted plaques <br>• Hyperkeratotic skin <br>• Often in immunocompromised patients | • Extreme form of scabies <br>• Mites present in large numbers <br>• Requires combination therapy (oral + topical) |
